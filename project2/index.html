Design decisions and rationale

Scores run from −2 fully deceptive to +2 fully earnest. Each score reflects the choice’s benefits and limitations.

Municipal Progress Review

4 decisions

A fictional statistical bulletin for city planners and officials who manage development budgets. It uses consistent measurements and a formal style to appear trustworthy to these readers.

  1. −1.5

    Judge progress by the falling share.

    The headline and percentage bars make the smaller share the main evidence of improvement. This is a reasonable way to ask how common these conditions are, but it leaves out the growing number of residents affected. We considered showing both measures together, which would give readers more context but weaken this argument. The negative score reflects that omission. The percentages are accurate, but a reader could take them as evidence that the housing problem became smaller overall.

  2. +2

    Use the same countries and population weights.

    Every country has both measures in all four years shown. We add the estimated counts and divide by the combined urban population to find the overall share. This means a change in which countries have data cannot explain the result. Simply averaging the country percentages would give a small country the same influence as a large one. We chose population weights because the chart describes residents. The positive score reflects this consistent calculation, although the sample excludes countries with missing records.

  3. +2

    Use bars and show every complete year.

    We replaced the circle grids with bars starting at zero because their lengths make the differences easier to compare. Every bar uses the full 0–100% scale. We include all four years with complete data for the original sample, rather than choosing years by whether they support the argument. The 2018 increase over 2014 remains visible and is stated in the caption. We considered a line chart, but separate bars avoid suggesting that we know what happened in the gaps between observations.

  4. −0.5

    Keep attention on the long-term decline.

    The muted green, serif headline, and small publication name are meant to resemble a statistical bulletin a planner might use. The caption compares 2018 with 2000, drawing attention to the overall improvement while also acknowledging the rise after 2014. We considered giving the recent increase the headline, which would tell a different story about the same record. We give the style a slightly negative score because an official-looking presentation can make this selected emphasis seem more trustworthy, even with a fictional publisher.

The Housing Ledger

4 decisions

A fictional housing newspaper for tenant advocates, legal-aid staff, and people who provide city services. It focuses on how many residents still need better housing, a concern these readers would bring to the data.

  1. −1.5

    Judge progress by the number still affected.

    The headline and caption ask readers to judge progress by whether fewer residents still live in slum conditions. The count rose by 25.3%, but the publication leaves out the falling percentage and the growth in the urban population. We considered putting the percentage beside the count, which would make the other interpretation easier to see. The negative score reflects this selective argument. More people needing better housing is a reason for concern, but it does not prove there was no improvement.

  2. +1.5

    Calculate country estimates before adding them.

    We multiply each country’s reported percentage by its own urban population, divide by 100, and add the estimates. This keeps each percentage matched to the population it describes. Using a World percentage instead could introduce countries outside our sample. The calculation shows how many residents are affected, but countries define urban areas differently and the source gives no range of uncertainty. We label the results as estimates and round the displayed totals. The positive score recognizes the careful calculation while allowing for those limits.

  3. +2

    Compare four totals from the same zero baseline.

    Each bar shows the full count in that year. Brown consistently represents the 2000 count, and red shows the increase above it. We added a color key before the bars so readers can understand the red section immediately. The same four years appear in both publications, and all lengths use unrounded values on a 0–800 million scale. This replaces a single bar whose parts required more explanation. The red section is a difference in estimated counts, not a tracked group of residents.

  4. −0.5

    Draw attention to the increase in red.

    The heavier newspaper title and red addition make the growing count stand out as a housing problem that still needs attention. The color changes what readers notice first without changing the amount shown. The caption also explains that the bar does not follow the same people over time. Using one color would give the increase less emphasis, while a brighter warning color could make the argument feel exaggerated. The slightly negative score reflects how the design draws attention to growing need while leaving out the falling percentage.

The evidence

The full housing account

Source, definitions & calculations

We use the urban-development dataset, revision f6bc043, from the September 15, 2021 World Bank export. The sample includes every country with both required measures in both 2000 and 2018. We exclude totals for the world, regions, and income groups. We do not treat missing values as zeros or fill them in using nearby years.

Both charts show every year from 2000 through 2018 with both measures for all 49 countries. These years are 2000, 2005, 2014, and 2018. We omit 2010 because seven countries are missing and 2016 because China is missing. The other years have no complete country observations for these measures. The bars compare separate observations and do not imply a steady change between them. The summary table and country tallies above compare the two endpoints.

The source’s “slum” measure concerns households lacking specified conditions such as water, sanitation, space, durable housing, or tenure security. It does not capture every kind of housing insecurity. Urban definitions differ between countries.

Country estimate = urban population × reported share ÷ 100
Panel prevalence = sum of country estimates ÷ total urban population × 100

We round values only when displaying them. The source gives no range showing how far the estimates might be from the true counts. Comparing these estimates does not follow the same people over time or show that a particular policy caused the change. The totals cover our sample, not the whole world.

Reflection

Choosing a standard of progress

Before choosing our proposition, we explored the World Bank data to understand which measures were available, what they described, and whether we could compare the same countries over time. We chose housing because we could combine the percentage living in slum conditions with the urban population to estimate how many people were affected. We used the 49 countries with both measures in both 2000 and 2018 so that both arguments would describe the same places. Once we had checked the measures and available years, calculating the counts was straightforward. We multiplied each country’s urban population by its reported percentage, divided by 100, and added the results. For the overall percentage, we used population weights so that each country’s influence reflected its number of urban residents. The share fell from 39.7% to 30.0%, while the count rose from 617.9 million to 774.5 million. What stood out was that 32 countries experienced both changes individually. This meant the disagreement was also present within countries, rather than being explained only by how we combined them. It gave us a reason to ask what “substantial progress” should mean while keeping our claim limited to the countries and years we studied.

The harder part was making each argument persuasive while keeping the charts easy to understand. We wrote Municipal Progress Review for city planners and budget officials who might judge progress by how common these conditions are. We initially used two grids of circles, but feedback pointed out that the difference was hard to see. We replaced them with bars and added every intermediate year with complete data for the same countries. This also revealed that the share rose after 2014, which an endpoint comparison did not show. We kept that change visible. We wrote The Housing Ledger for advocates and service providers concerned with how many people still need better housing. Its original single bar made the red section difficult to interpret at first glance. Showing a full bar for each available year makes the rising totals easier to compare, and the color key explains that red means the increase above the 2000 count. Both publications now use the same four years. We left out years with missing countries rather than filling gaps or changing the sample. The publication names, fonts, colors, and headlines still give each side a reason to appear credible to its audience, while the explanations below the charts identify what each argument leaves out.

This comparison showed us that correct numbers can still lead readers toward an incomplete conclusion. The falling share is evidence of improvement, while the growing count shows that more residents still experienced these conditions. Neither measure answers the whole question on its own. What surprised us was that we could make both arguments without changing a value or cutting off a scale. Choosing one measure and leaving out the other already did much of the work. That is why we gave negative scores to those choices and positive scores to the consistent sample, accurate marks, and clear calculations. We define ethical analysis and visualization as choosing measures that fit the question, explaining how they were calculated, and making claims the evidence can support. A publication can focus on percentages or counts, but readers should be able to find the information needed to question its argument. Our shared evidence section and downloadable country records make that possible, although we cannot assume every reader will look at them. We would draw a clear boundary at inventing quotes, replacing missing values with zeros, switching countries between arguments, calling this sample a global count, or claiming a policy caused changes that these data cannot explain. We identify both publishers as fictional and label the counts as estimates. Those choices help readers understand what the project can and cannot establish.
